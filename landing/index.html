Does it work?

About 4× better than best-sellers.

A simulated new visitor likes 3 of a real shopper's purchases. The model then has to find the rest of what that shopper bought in the following two weeks. Tested on 8,959 shoppers it never saw while learning.

recall@12: the share of what the shopper really bought next that appears in the model's top 12. MAP@12: the same, but it also rewards putting those items near the top. New designs only: leaving out other colours of a design the shopper had already liked, so "the same item in another colour" can't count as a win.

Every gap is well clear of chance: the 95% bootstrap intervals don't overlap (recommender 0.139–0.152, best-sellers 0.030–0.036).

How it works

Three steps, and every card explains itself.

STEP 01

You swipe

Like, dislike, or "not sure". Your likes are the only input: no profile, no quiz. Until your third like you see best-sellers.

STEP 02

It compares 128 numbers

From millions of purchases, the model learned 128 numbers for every garment: pieces bought by the same people end up close together. Your taste is the average of what you liked, and every garment is ranked by how close it sits.

STEP 03

Every card says why

Tap ⓘ: "Because you liked the black hoodie", with the match score. Two cards in every ten are deliberate wildcards, so the feed doesn't narrow to one lane.

What it isn't

Honest limits.

  • A research dataset, not a shop. The garments are from H&M's public dataset; prices are made up, and nothing can be bought.
  • "Bought together" isn't "goes together". The model learned from what people bought, not from what looks good in one outfit.
  • Dislikes are a guess. The dataset has purchases but no dislikes, so how much a ✕ pushes the feed away is an educated guess, not something it learned.
  • Tested offline. The numbers above come from replaying past shoppers, not from live visitors.
  • Mostly womenswear, because that's most of the catalogue.
